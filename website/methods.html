ALTO assigns Alaska's daily aircraft turns at Seattle‑Tacoma to gates, prices what a disruption costs, and measures how much of that re-planning gets back. This page covers where the data came from, how the model is put together, the mistakes I made building it, and what it cannot tell you.

1 · The data

Every flight comes from the U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics Airline On-Time Performance dataset for 2023, filtered to carrier code AS with either origin or destination SEA. That is 139,000 flight records, of which 155,246 leg-records survive once cancellations and diversions are removed.

I used BTS because it records actual wheels-down and pushback times. Gate occupancy depends on when an aircraft really arrived.

All times become absolute minutes since 1 January 00:00. BTS stores clock times as HHMM integers, so comparing 1438 on the 15th against 0410 on the 16th gives the wrong order, and every overnight turn turns into a special case. One integer per moment removes that whole class of bug.

Arrivals crossing midnight need correcting. BTS stamps a flight with its departure date, so a red-eye landing at 04:10 records that time against the previous day. I catch it by looking for an arrival clock time earlier than its departure clock time.

Does this look like Seattle?

Before trusting anything the model produced, I checked the shape of the data against what the airport publishes. The Port of Seattle says August is its busiest travel month, and that TSA expects the busiest checkpoint days to be Sundays, Mondays, Thursdays and Fridays. Both are outside checks. Neither went into building anything.

TSA's busiest day nationally in 2023 was the Sunday after Thanksgiving, at 2.9 million passengers. In this dataset Thanksgiving Day is the quietest day of the year. Both can be true at once. The busiest single day here is 28 July, at 267 visits. The margin is not worth much. The top ten days run 267 down to 263 and all of them fall in late July or August, so which one ranks first changed when I added arrival-only and departure-only visits. Rank by gate blocks and 7 August leads with 382. Rank by fees and 7 August leads again. Rank by stands needed and it is 27 July with 36. The model page says as much rather than naming a single date.

2 · Building turns

A turn is one aircraft's ground visit. It lands, sits at a gate, departs. My first version built turns with a pandas merge on tail number and date, which is a cartesian join.

One aircraft, one day

Tail N434AS came through Seattle twice on 15 July 2023:

TimeEvent
14:38arrives from JFK
15:51departs for SFO
21:47arrives from SFO
23:28departs for JFK

Two visits. The merge produced four pairings, and my follow-up filter caught only one of the bad ones.

PairingGround timeVerdict
14:38 → 15:5173 minreal
14:38 → 23:28530 mininvented, and kept
21:47 → 15:51—removed by the time filter
21:47 → 23:28101 minreal

So the model had N434AS holding a gate from mid-afternoon until nearly midnight while the aircraft was in San Francisco. Across 2023 that turned 75,347 real turns into 79,052, pushed the 75th-percentile turn from 106 minutes to 305, and drove peak gate demand on 15 July from 29 up to 76. Alaska has 57 gates, so the assignment problem had no solution at all, and the solver refused to give one.

The fix walks each aircraft's timeline in order and pairs every arrival with the next unclaimed departure by the same tail, taking each departure out of the pool once it is used. The phantom turn never gets built in the first place.

I found it by checking the turn-time distribution against how long a 737 actually sits at a gate. A 75th percentile of five hours is not a turnaround.

The tariff supports the 36-hour cutoff. The Port of Seattle charges $200 per 12-hour period for overnight parking and $5,000 per 12-hour period beyond 24 hours. Aircraft do not sit.

Aircraft that only arrive, or only depart

Defining a turn as an arrival paired with a departure dropped a whole category of gate use. An aircraft that ends its day in Seattle has no departure to pair with, and one that starts its day here has no arrival. Both take a gate.

That is roughly 12.5 gate uses a day the model could not see, and it explains why the flight picker only ever offered aircraft passing through Seattle.

They are built as arrival-only and departure-only visits now. Their lengths come from the tariff, which publishes separate maximum gate occupancies for both cases: 60 minutes for an arrival-only visit and 75 for a departure-only one, at 100 to 199 seats.

After the rebuild the dataset holds 79,899 visits and 108,050 occupancy blocks. Peak stand demand runs between 23 and 39 a day, median 31, with no day past the 73-stand roster. The three methods still agree on all 16 days tested.

3 · Where aircraft park

Seattle-Tacoma publishes a maximum gate occupancy. The numbers in this section come from that document.

AircraftTurnaroundArrival onlyDeparture only
400+ seats180 min75120
200–399 seats150 min6090
100–199 seats, Alaska's 737-800 and -900120 min6075
Under 100 seats90 min4545

Past the limit the airport charges a gate delay fee for every 15-minute increment: $250 for the first 90 minutes over, $500 up to four hours over, $1,000 after that. An aircraft left at a gate overnight would owe tens of thousands of dollars.

Industry practice says the same thing in plainer words. An aircraft going out of service for the day is parked on a remote area of the airport, or at least pushed back away from the boarding gate. The procedure is called Remain Overnight.

So a turn longer than the published maximum becomes two gate blocks. The arrival, at the tariff's 60-minute arrival-only limit. The aircraft towed away. Then a return to board, at the 75-minute departure-only limit.

What parking away costs

The tariff charges for it in steps.

up to 4 hours           $100   remote hardstand fee
each of the next two    $200   per 12-hour period
every period after that $5,000   per 12-hour period

That last step is punishing enough to stop carriers using Sea-Tac as a car park, and it means two days of sitting costs vastly more than six hours. My first version charged a flat $100 and lost all of it.

A rule I got wrong

For about a day this model towed only when another aircraft needed the gate, on the reasoning that at three in the morning nobody wants it. I never checked that reasoning against a source, and it was wrong. The tariff's occupancy limits and its Remain Overnight schedule are both built around aircraft not sitting at gates.

The evidence that seemed damning at the time was gates standing empty overnight while remote stands filled up. That is what a hub looks like at five in the morning.

Every parameter now carries its source in docs/verification.md, marked verified, derived or assumed. Two are marked partially verified, and both say what is still open.

4 · The model

Two solvers run the same problem. One is fast enough to sit behind a slider. The other is the literal statement of the problem, and I use it to check the first.

The integer program

Variables
  x[b,g] = 1 if block b is parked at gate g, else 0
  y[g]   = 1 if gate g is used at all that day, else 0

Minimize   F · Σb,g∈S x[b,g] + M · Σg y[g] + Σb,g walk[g] · x[b,g]

Subject to
  Σg x[b,g] = 1             ∀ b    every block gets exactly one gate
  Σb∈Q x[b,g] ≤ y[g]    ∀ Q, g  overlapping blocks cannot share a gate
  y[g] ≥ y[g+1]           ∀ g    symmetry breaking

The three terms are ranked rather than blended. S is the set of common-use stands, billed per turn, so F comes first. M sits above the largest walking cost a day could incur, so no amount of walking justifies an extra gate. Walking decides what is left.

Two things make it tractable. Q ranges over maximal groups of mutually overlapping blocks rather than over pairs, which works because gate occupancy forms an interval graph and every group of overlapping blocks is already fully present when one of them starts. On 15 July that is 125 group constraints in place of 4,398 pairwise ones, and each one is tighter. Symmetry breaking then stops the solver proving that thousands of gate relabellings are equally good.

The network flow

Forget gates and think about chains. A gate holds one aircraft, then another, then another. So the fewest gates is the same question as the fewest chains covering every block, which is a minimum-cost path cover, which is a network flow, which solves in milliseconds.

each block becomes two nodes, a front door and a back door

SOURCE → in[i]    open a gate for block i    cost: high
out[i] → in[j]    j follows i on the same gate cost: idle minutes
out[i] → SINK     i is last on its gate       cost: 0
SINK → SOURCE     the return pipe, whose flow is the gate count

Each block takes exactly one unit into its front door and one out of its back door, which forces the result into clean non-overlapping chains. The Hungarian algorithm then pairs chains with named gates, weighing the common-use fee first, how many aircraft would have to move second, and walking last.

A 10-minute buffer between consecutive aircraft at a gate is applied identically in the conflict test, the flow network and the peak-demand count. Different buffers in different places would make the comparison meaningless.

5 · Validation

A millisecond answer is only worth having if it is also the right answer. Three independent methods run on the same days:

  • Sweep-line peak demand, the most aircraft on the ground at once. A hard lower bound, and because gate occupancy is an interval graph, the exact minimum as well.
  • Minimum-cost network flow, the production solver.
  • The integer program, solved exactly.

All 16 days agree on the number of gates. They cover quiet February weekdays, July 4th, Thanksgiving, Christmas Eve and the busiest day in the dataset. Network flow averages 0.3 seconds against the integer program's 12, so 24× to 60× faster.

Where the fast solver is worse

Gate count is optimal in both. Walking cost is 9.3% worse in the network flow, on all 16 days.

The cause is structural. Flow treats every gate as interchangeable, so it builds chains first and assigns gate names afterwards, while the integer program chooses chains and names together and reaches arrangements flow cannot. The chain-to-gate pairing is already provably optimal given the chains, so the gap lives in the chain structure and per-gate costs cannot influence it.

The same structure costs more than walking when gates are scarce. A chain cannot be split, so shutting 39 gates leaves 18 leased stands against 31 chains, and thirteen chains land on common-use stands dragging every turn they contain with them. On 15 July that put 83 turns on common-use stands where placing blocks one at a time put 50. The integer program has no chain constraint and got the same day to 48. Measured on that day, the flow solver's re-plan came out $12,439 worse than improvising, and the integer program's came out $7,306 better.

So the model page offers a Solve exactly control, and when the fast solver cannot beat the improvised plan it says so and keeps the improvised one.

6 · The cost model

ALTO prices three things: delay, aircraft parking and towing, and common-use gate charges. Those are the costs a gate plan can change.

It leaves out fuel, crew, maintenance, landing fees, ground handling and the rent on Alaska's own gates. Those are real money and much larger, and they are also the same whichever plan you pick, so adding them would put a constant on every scenario and say nothing about which plan is better. Every figure on this site is a difference between plans, and every component is something an airline gets invoiced for.

The $98.41 breaks down as crew $37.01, fuel $29.34, maintenance $18.35, aircraft ownership $9.76 and other $3.95. It is the airline's own cost. A4A separately values passenger time at roughly $47 an hour, which ALTO leaves out, so every disruption figure here sits on the low side.

The propagation factor has no source at all, which is why it is a slider.

Why idle gate time is no longer priced

It used to be, at a derived $6.14 a minute, the one figure in the cost model I could not quote from a tariff. It produced a result that cannot be true. On 17 June, closing gates N1, N11 and N20 gave a disruption cost of minus $682, so closing gates appeared to save money.

Idle was measured as the gaps between consecutive aircraft at the gates in use, so fewer gates in use meant fewer gaps to count. Neither solver minimises idle time, which means it was never being optimised, only observed, and an improvised plan after a closure could beat the optimal one on it. Idle was the largest term at $105k of $146k that day, so it swamped everything else.

Underneath that, idle time is not a charge. Rent on a preferential gate is fixed by the lease and does not move when the plan changes, so putting it into a comparison of plans adds a large number that carries no information about the decision. Everything left is something an airline is invoiced for. Gate use is reported as a utilisation percentage.

7 · What a disruption costs

Multiplying injected delay by cost per minute would miss where the money goes. A late arrival takes a gate promised to someone else, that aircraft holds, departs late, arrives late down-line, and is late back at Seattle in the evening. One late arrival becomes six late departures.

So the damage figure is a simulation. The day runs forward with the gate plan held fixed, under two forces that feed each other:

  • Gate contention. The gate you were promised is still occupied, so you wait.
  • Rotation carry. Your aircraft arrived late, so your departure is late. Slack absorbs part of it and the rest carries forward.

Since each makes the other worse, this iterates rather than running once, following the delay one leg further each pass, capped at four legs. Past four the effect is smaller than the error in the estimate of it.

Doing nothing, when a gate is shut

An aircraft whose gate has closed has to go somewhere. My first version dropped it from the plan, and a dropped aircraft costs nothing, so closing six gates appeared to save $26,207.

The comparison that means something is optimising against what a gate controller does under pressure. Take the displaced aircraft in time order and put each one at the cheapest stand that is free, leased gates before common-use ones, with no lookahead. Anything that fits nowhere goes to a remote hardstand and pays the tariff for it.

I tried sending all of them straight to hardstands, which cost $100 each against $552.89 for a common-use turn, and that made doing nothing cheaper than re-planning. A hardstand is not a substitute for a gate. An aircraft parked on one cannot board, so it is where an aircraft ends up when no stand is left, rather than a cheaper option somebody would choose.

One more rule holds the comparison together. Closing a gate cannot make another aircraft leave earlier. Without it, taking aircraft off gates removed them from gate contention, the simulated delay for the day fell, and a bigger closure came out cheaper than a smaller one. The direction is certain even though the size is not, so the damage run keeps whichever departure time is later, its own or the one that block had with nothing closed.

Minimum-disruption recovery

A recovery plan that saves marginally more but reshuffles two hundred aircraft is not something operations can execute. Two mechanisms keep the answer usable. The chain-to-gate pairing counts how many aircraft would leave their original gate, and links used in the previous plan get a small discount inside the flow itself, so among equally good arrangements the solver returns the one closest to the plan already in place.

On a three-aircraft delay that took the number of aircraft moved from 265 down to 56, at a cost of 2.3 percentage points of recovered share. I took that trade deliberately.

8 · What this cannot tell you

Regional flying is missing

Horizon Air and SkyWest fly Embraer E175s in Alaska livery and use Alaska's gates. Neither can be included, for two different reasons.

Horizon does not file BTS On-Time Performance at all. The 2023 files contain exactly fifteen carriers: 9E, AA, AS, B6, DL, F9, G4, HA, MQ, NK, OH, OO, UA, WN and YX. Horizon is absent, so its aircraft are invisible here and nothing can recover them.

SkyWest is present and cannot be attributed. 3,170 SkyWest flights touched SEA in January 2023 against Alaska mainline's 11,605, and SkyWest flies for Alaska, Delta, United and American at once while this table records only the operating carrier. I tried three ways to attribute them and all three failed. There is no marketing-carrier or codeshare field. Flight-number blocks do not separate partners, since both the 3000- and 4000-series serve Pacific Northwest routes from SEA. Aircraft do not segregate either: 103 of the 104 SkyWest tails that touched SEA in January also touched another mainline's hub.

Attributing them by route heuristic would produce a plausible-looking number with nothing behind it, so I left the gap in place and stated it. Every utilisation figure here is a floor for Alaska mainline rather than an estimate of the Alaska brand's total gate demand. Closing it properly means BTS's separate Marketing Carrier table, which carries both operating and marketing carrier, and even that would leave Horizon out.

The rest

No public dataset contains gate assignments. Which gate a flight actually used is unknowable from public data, so ALTO produces an optimal assignment rather than a reconstruction of the real one, and there is no ground truth to check it against. The solvers are validated against each other and against theory, which is what section 5 does.

The gate roster is a modelling assumption. Seattle‑Tacoma has 67 preferential-use gates under its current airline agreement, and Concourse C and the North Satellite are Alaska's. The exact roster, and the walking-cost weights on each gate, are built from the published terminal layout rather than measured.

2023 flights are priced at 2025 rates. The tariff and agreement figures are current and the flights are two years older, which is fine for relative comparisons and worth stating.

Delay costs leave out passenger time, which A4A values at about $47 an hour. Including it would raise every disruption figure substantially.
