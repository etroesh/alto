ALTO assigns Alaska's daily aircraft turns at Seattle‑Tacoma to gates, prices what a disruption costs, and measures how much of that re-planning gets back. This page covers where the data came from, how the model is put together, the mistakes I made building it, and what it cannot tell you.

1 · The data

Every flight comes from the U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics Airline On-Time Performance dataset for 2023, filtered to carrier code AS with either origin or destination SEA. That leaves 139,000 flight records and 155,246 leg-records once cancelled and diverted flights are excluded.

I picked BTS because it records actual wheels-down and wheels-up times. Gate occupancy depends on exact arrival times.

Clock times are converted to absolute minutes since 1 January 00:00. BTS records clock times as HHMM integers, so comparing 1438 on the 15th against 0410 on the 16th gives the wrong order, and every overnight turn becomes a special case. One integer per moment solves the problem.

Overnight arrivals need correcting. A flight is stamped with its departure date, so a red-eye landing at 04:10 gets recorded against the previous day. I catch it by checking whether the arrival clock time comes before the departure clock time.

Does this look like Seattle?

Before trusting anything the model produced, I checked whether the shape of the data matched what the airport reports. The Port of Seattle names August as its busiest travel month, and TSA expects the busiest checkpoint days to be Sundays, Mondays, Thursdays and Fridays. Both are outside checks. Neither went into building anything.

The four busiest weekdays here are the four the airport names, and the three quietest are the three it does not. The months line up the same way: August is the most crowded at 254.9 visits a day, February the least at 187.1.

TSA's busiest day nationally in 2023 was the Sunday after Thanksgiving, at 2.9 million passengers. In this dataset, Thanksgiving Day is the quietest day of the year. Both can be true. The holiday peak is fuller aircraft; the summer peak is more aircraft — and a gate is taken by an aircraft, not a passenger.

The busiest single day here is 28 July, at 267 visits. The lead is not worth much: the top ten busiest days run from 267 down to 263, and all of them fall in July or August. That is why the ranking moves once arrival-only and departure-only visits are counted. Rank by gate blocks and 7 August leads with 382. Rank by fees and 7 August leads again. Rank by stands needed and it is 27 July, with 36. The model page states the range rather than naming a single date.

2 · Building turns

A turn is an aircraft's time on the ground: it arrives, sits at the gate, and leaves. My first attempt built turns with a pandas merge on tail number and date — a cartesian join.

One aircraft, one day

Tail N434AS came through Seattle twice on 15 July 2023:

TimeEvent
14:38arrives from JFK
15:51departs for SFO
21:47arrives from SFO
23:28departs for JFK

Two visits. The merge produced four pairings, and my follow-up filter caught only one of the bad ones.

PairingGround timeVerdict
14:38 → 15:5173 minreal
14:38 → 23:28530 mininvented, and kept
21:47 → 15:51—removed by the time filter
21:47 → 23:28101 minreal

So the model had N434AS guarding a gate from mid-afternoon until nearly midnight while the aircraft was in San Francisco. Across 2023 that turned 75,347 real turns into 79,052, pushed the 75th-percentile turn from 106 minutes to 305, and drove peak gate demand on 15 July from 29 up to 76. Alaska has only 57 gates, so the assignment problem had no solution at all — there was no answer to give.

The fix walks each aircraft's schedule in order and pairs every arrival with the next unused departure by the same tail, taking each departure out of the pool once it is used. The phantom turn never gets built in the first place.

I caught it by checking the distribution of turn times against how long a 737 actually sits on the ground. A 75th percentile of five hours is not a turnaround.

The tariff supports the 36-hour cutoff. The Port of Seattle charges $200 per 12-hour period for overnight parking and $5,000 per 12-hour period beyond 24 hours. Aircraft do not sit.

Aircraft that only arrive, or only depart

Defining a turn as an arrival paired with a departure dropped a whole category of gate use. An aircraft that ends its day in Seattle has no departure to pair with, and one that starts its day here has no arrival. Both take a gate.

That is roughly 12.5 gate uses a day the model could not see, which is why the flight picker only ever offered aircraft that passed all the way through Seattle.

They are now built as arrival-only and departure-only visits. Their length comes from the tariff, which publishes a different maximum for each: 60 minutes for an arrival-only visit, 75 for a departure-only one, at 100 to 199 seats.

After the rebuild the dataset holds 79,899 visits and 108,050 occupancy blocks. Peak stand demand runs between 23 and 39 a day, median 31, with no day past the 73-stand roster. The three methods still agree, on all 16 days tested.

3 · Where aircraft park

Seattle-Tacoma publishes a maximum gate occupancy. The numbers in this section come from that document.

AircraftTurnaroundArrival onlyDeparture only
400+ seats180 min75120
200–399 seats150 min6090
100–199 seats, Alaska's 737-800 and -900120 min6075
Under 100 seats90 min4545

Past the limit the airport charges a gate delay fee in $250 increments for every 15 minutes: $250 for the first hour and a half over, $500 for the next two and a half hours, and $1,000 after that. An aircraft left at a gate overnight would owe tens of thousands of dollars.

Industry practice says this more simply. An aircraft going out of service for the night gets parked in a remote area of the airport, or at least moved away from the boarding gate. The procedure is called Remain Overnight.

So a turn longer than the published maximum becomes two gate blocks. The arrival, at the 60-minute arrival-only limit. The aircraft towed away. The departure, at the 75-minute departure-only limit.

What parking away costs

The tariff charges for it in steps.

up to 4 hours           $100   remote hardstand fee
each of the next two    $200   per 12-hour period
every period after that $5,000   per 12-hour period

That last step is punishing enough to stop carriers using Sea-Tac as a car park, and it means two days of sitting costs vastly more than six hours. My first version charged a flat $100 and lost all of it.

A rule I got wrong

For about a day this model towed an aircraft only when another one needed the gate, on the reasoning that nobody wants it at three in the morning. I never checked that reasoning against a source, and it was wrong: the tariff's occupancy limits and its Remain Overnight schedule are both built around aircraft not sitting at gates.

The evidence that looked damning at the time was gates standing empty overnight while remote stands filled up. That is exactly what a hub looks like at five in the morning.

Every parameter now carries its source in docs/verification.md, marked verified, derived or assumed. Two are marked partially verified, and both say what is still unresolved.

4 · The model

Two solvers run the same problem. One is fast enough to sit behind a slider. The other is the literal statement of the problem, and I use it to check the first.

The integer program

Variables
  x[b,g] = 1 if block b is parked at gate g, else 0
  y[g]   = 1 if gate g is used at all that day, else 0

Minimize   F · Σb,g∈S x[b,g] + M · Σg y[g] + Σb,g walk[g] · x[b,g]

Subject to
  Σg x[b,g] = 1             ∀ b    every block gets exactly one gate
  Σb∈Q x[b,g] ≤ y[g]    ∀ Q, g  overlapping blocks cannot share a gate
  y[g] ≥ y[g+1]           ∀ g    symmetry breaking

The three terms are ranked rather than blended. S is the set of common-use stands, priced by turn, so F takes precedence over M. M in turn outranks the largest possible walking cost in a day, so no amount of walking justifies an extra gate. Walking decides what is left.

Two things make it tractable. Q ranges over maximal groups of mutually overlapping blocks rather than over pairs, because gate occupancy forms an interval graph and every group of mutually overlapping blocks is a subset of one of these maximal groups. On 15 July that is 125 group constraints in place of 4,398 pairwise ones, and each one is tighter. Symmetry breaking then stops the solver from proving that thousands of gate relabellings are equally good solutions.

The network flow

Forget gates and think about chains. A gate holds one aircraft, then another, then another. So the fewest gates is the same question as the fewest chains covering every block — a minimum-cost path cover, which is a network flow problem.

each block becomes two nodes, a front door and a back door

SOURCE → in[i]    open a gate for block i    cost: high
out[i] → in[j]    j follows i on the same gate cost: idle minutes
out[i] → SINK     i is last on its gate       cost: 0
SINK → SOURCE     the return pipe, whose flow is the gate count

Each block takes exactly one unit in through its front door and sends one out through its back door, which forces the result into clean, non-overlapping chains. The Hungarian algorithm then matches these chains to named gates, weighing the common-use fee first, how many aircraft would have to move second, and walking last.

A 10-minute buffer between consecutive aircraft at a gate is applied identically in the conflict test, the flow network and the peak-demand count. A different buffer in different places would make the comparison meaningless.

5 · Validation

An answer in a millisecond is only useful if it is also the right one. Three independent methods run on every day tested:

  • Sweep-line peak demand, the most aircraft on the ground at once. A hard lower bound, and because gate occupancy is an interval graph, the exact minimum as well.
  • Minimum-cost network flow, the production solver.
  • The integer program, solved exactly.

All 16 days agree on the number of gates. They cover quiet February weekdays, July 4th, Thanksgiving, Christmas Eve and the busiest day in the dataset. Network flow averages 0.3 seconds against the integer program's 12 — 24× to 60× faster, depending on the day.

Where the fast solver is worse

Gate count is optimal in both solvers. Walking cost is 9.3% higher in the network flow, on all 16 days.

The cause is structural. Flow treats every gate as interchangeable, so it builds chains first and assigns gate names afterward, while the integer program chooses chains and names together and reaches arrangements flow cannot. The chain-to-gate pairing is already provably optimal given the chains, so the gap has to be in the chain structure itself, not in per-gate cost.

The same structure costs more than walking when gates are scarce. A chain cannot be split, so closing 39 gates leaves 18 leased stands against 31 chains, and thirteen of those chains land on common-use stands, carrying every turn they contain with them. On 15 July that put 83 turns on common-use stands, against 50 when blocks are placed one at a time. The integer program has no chain restriction and got the same day down to 48. Measured on that day, the flow solver's re-plan came out $12,439 more expensive than improvising, and the integer program's came out $7,306 cheaper.

So the model page offers a Solve exactly control, and when the fast solver cannot beat the improvised plan, it says so and keeps the improvised plan instead.

6 · The cost model

ALTO prices three things: delay, aircraft parking and towing, and common-use gate charges. Those are the costs a gate plan can change.

It leaves out fuel, crew, maintenance, landing fees, ground handling and the rent on Alaska's own gates. Those are real money and much larger, and they are also the same whichever plan you pick, so adding them would put a constant on every scenario and say nothing about which plan is better. Every figure on this site is a difference between plans, and every component is something an airline gets invoiced for.

The $98.41 breaks down as $37.01 for crew, $29.34 for fuel, $18.35 for maintenance, $9.76 for aircraft ownership and $3.95 for other costs. It is the airline's own cost. A4A separately values passenger time at roughly $47 an hour; ALTO leaves that out, so every disruption figure here sits on the low side.

The propagation factor has no source at all, which is why it is a slider.

Why idle gate time is no longer priced

It used to be priced, at a derived $6.14 a minute — the one figure in the cost model I could not quote from a tariff. It produced a result that cannot be true: closing gates N1, N11 and N20 on 17 June gave a disruption cost of minus $682, so closing gates appeared to save money.

Idle was calculated as the gap between consecutive aircraft at the gates in use, so closing gates reduced the amount of idle time to count. Neither solver minimises idle time — it was never being optimised, only observed — so an improvised plan after a closure could beat the optimal one on it. Idle was the largest component of that day's cost, $105k of $146k total.

Underneath that, idle time is not a charge. Rent on a preferential gate is fixed by the lease and does not change with the plan, so putting it into a comparison of plans adds a large number with no information in it for the decision. Everything else on this page is something an airline is actually invoiced for. Gate use is instead reported as a utilisation rate.

7 · What a disruption costs

Multiplying an injected delay by the cost per minute would miss where the money actually goes. A late arrival takes a gate promised to another aircraft, which then holds, departs late, arrives late at its own destination, and comes back late to Seattle that evening. One late arrival becomes six late departures.

So the damage figure comes from a simulation, run forward through the day with the gate plan held fixed, under two forces that feed each other:

  • Gate contention — the gate you were promised is still occupied, so you wait.
  • Rotation carry — your aircraft arrived late, so you leave late too; slack absorbs part of it and the rest carries forward.

Since each makes the other worse, this runs as a loop rather than once, carrying the delay one leg further each pass, capped at four legs — past that point the effect is smaller than the error in estimating it.

Doing nothing, when a gate is shut

An aircraft whose gate has closed still has to go somewhere. My first version simply dropped it from the plan, and a dropped aircraft costs nothing — so closing six gates appeared to save $26,207.

The comparison that means something is the one that mimics what a gate controller does under pressure: take the displaced aircraft in time order and put each one at the cheapest stand that is free, leased gates before common-use ones, with no lookahead. Anything that fits nowhere goes to a remote hardstand and pays the tariff for it.

I tried sending all of them straight to hardstands, which cost $100 against $552.89 for a common-use turn, and that made doing nothing cheaper than re-planning. A hardstand is not a substitute for a gate: an aircraft parked on one cannot board passengers, so it is where an aircraft ends up when there is no stand left, not a cheaper option anyone would actually choose.

One more rule ties the comparison together: closing a gate cannot make another aircraft leave earlier. Without it, taking aircraft off gates removed them from gate contention, the simulated delay for the day fell, and closing more gates came out cheaper than closing fewer. The direction is certain even where the size is not, so the damage run keeps whichever departure time is later — its own, or the one that block would have had with nothing closed.

Minimum-disruption recovery

A recovery plan that saves a little more but reshuffles two hundred aircraft is not something operations can actually run. Two mechanisms keep the answer usable. The chain-to-gate pairing counts how many aircraft would leave their original gate, and links that match the previous plan get a small discount inside the flow itself, so among equally good arrangements the solver favors the one closest to what was already in place.

On a three-aircraft delay, that took the number of aircraft moved from 265 down to 56, at a cost of 2.3 percentage points of recovered share. I made that trade deliberately.

8 · What this cannot tell you

Regional flying is missing

Horizon Air and SkyWest both fly Embraer E175s in Alaska livery and use Alaska's gates, and neither can be included — for two different reasons.

Horizon does not report BTS On-Time Performance at all. The 2023 files include exactly fifteen carriers — 9E, AA, AS, B6, DL, F9, G4, HA, MQ, NK, OH, OO, UA, WN and YX. No Horizon, so there are no Horizon aircraft to recover: they are simply invisible in this data.

SkyWest is reported but cannot be attributed. In January 2023 alone SkyWest flew 3,170 flights touching SEA against Alaska mainline's 11,605, and it flies for Alaska, Delta, United and American at once, while this table records only the operating carrier. I tried attributing them three ways and all three failed: there is no marketing-carrier or codeshare field; flight-number blocks do not separate partners, since both the 3000- and 4000-series serve Pacific Northwest routes out of SEA; and the aircraft themselves do not segregate by partner either — 103 of the 104 SkyWest tails that touched SEA in January also touched another mainline's hub.

A route heuristic would have produced a plausible-looking number with no data behind it, so I left the gap in place and stated it instead. Every utilisation figure here is a floor for Alaska mainline, not an estimate of the Alaska brand's total gate demand. Doing this properly would mean BTS's separate Marketing Carrier table, which carries both operating and marketing carrier — and even that would still leave Horizon out.

The rest

No public dataset contains gate assignments. There is no way to know which gate a flight actually used, so ALTO produces an optimal assignment rather than a reconstruction of the real one, with no ground truth to check it against. The solvers are validated against each other and against theory instead — that is what section 5 does.

The gate roster is a modelling assumption. Seattle‑Tacoma has 67 preferential-use gates under its current airline agreement, and Concourse C and the North Satellite belong to Alaska. The exact roster, and the walking-cost weight on each gate, are derived from the publicly available terminal map rather than measured.

2023 flights are priced at 2025 rates. The tariff and agreement figures are current while the flights are two years older — fine for relative comparisons, but worth stating plainly.

Disruption costs leave out the value of passenger time, which A4A puts at about $47 an hour. Including it would raise every disruption figure here, substantially.
